# PeatWatch versus North Sea flaring: the unusual data layer

Checked on **3 October 2026**. This is a research recommendation, not a product decision. Counts below are from live data queries and spatial joins made today; source datasets may change. No model improvement or live-alert accuracy has been measured yet.

## Recommendation

Choose **PeatWatch** for the CompSoc challenge. The surprising layer is [NatureScot's Peatland ACTION field survey](https://www.nature.scot/climate-change/nature-based-solutions/peatland-action/peatland-action-resources/peatland-action-open-data): actual, dated peat-depth measurements and condition labels, including artificial drainage and erosion. A satellite thermal dot can become a question about the **carbon-rich soil and drainage condition underneath or near it**. This fits the brief's small-compute and verifiable-result criteria: spatial joins, temporal clustering and a replay against an [official mapped fire perimeter](https://www.data.gov.uk/dataset/45ed6e75-fd12-46c8-965d-cc627889f39f/scottish-wildfire-and-muirburn-extents).

The specific demo question: **Which new or returning heat detections are near surveyed deep or drained peat, and where should someone inspect first?** The system should say “possible peatland fire / returning surface heat,” not “subsurface peat is burning.”

One concrete case file from the live joins: a VIIRS observation on **29 June 2025 at 02:09 UTC**, centred at **57.47860, −3.53366**, is **7 m** from a Peatland ACTION sample recorded in 2024 as **93 cm peat** and **`Drained: Artificial`**. That proximity connects a real prefire ground measurement to the heat observation. The VIIRS pixel is roughly **375 m**, so it does **not** prove the exact sample location burned or that 93 cm of peat was consumed.

Across the Dava observations, **57/372** thermal centres have a prefire field sample within 100 m, **99/372** within 375 m. Within the mapped fire perimeter are **1,471** prefire sample points; **950** measure over 40 cm peat and **615** carry the `Drained: Artificial` label. These describe **surveyed locations only**. The survey was designed around restoration projects, so its point frequencies cannot estimate the proportion of the whole burned area that was drained or deep peat.

The field paper says drainage histories were not identified for its Dava analysis. These NatureScot condition observations might add a partial field-based drainage proxy, but they must be evaluated for spatial coverage and selection bias. We tested direct matching to the paper's sampled burn-depth locations: only **6 of 112 raw location points** had a prefire Peatland ACTION sample within 100 m. Therefore a convincing per-point “drainage predicts burn depth” model is **not currently supported** by these two files alone.

### Buildable test, without overstating it

1. Use the current FIRMS feed for heat. Cluster points by distance and time and identify a return after a quiet interval. Show satellite/pass time and pixel footprint.
2. Join to NatureScot's coarse peatland map, then attach **actual field depth/condition only when a nearby dated survey exists**. Show distance to sample, date and “no nearby survey” elsewhere.
3. Rank candidate **inspection priority** from observed heat recurrence, peat depth, drainage/erosion label and weather/soil-moisture context. Start with a transparent rule or logistic regression, not an unexplained risk number.
4. Replay Dava, then test a **held-out** smaller fire/period. Score event detection versus a FIRMS-only baseline against mapped perimeters and count false alerts. Do not train and test on the same fire. NatureScot also maps a **2026 Cairngorms** event with **61** prefire survey points inside, a possible second event, though condition is `Not provided` for all 61.
5. Use the Dava burn-depth study to explain/validate the impact story, not to assert site-level emissions from thermal power alone.

The decisive experiment is whether the field layer improves **ranking of places worth inspecting** over a baseline that uses heat observations and coarse peatland polygons alone. Until that is measured, the unusual dataset is a promising angle, not a proven breakthrough.

## Best flaring alternative, and its limit

The strongest open flaring layer is the [Colorado School of Mines Earth Observation Group's annual VIIRS Nightfire gas-flare catalogue](https://eogdata.mines.edu/products/vnf/global_gas_flare.html). Its shortwave-infrared channel is tailored to flares that ordinary active-fire products can miss. We parsed the [2024 KML](https://eogdata.mines.edu/global_flare_data/2024_flare_summary_v20250730_j01.kml) and found a satellite flare centre **0.11 km** from the [NSTA Mariner flare stack](https://www.arcgis.com/home/item.html?id=a97ed4fcd8b34ef2b4527957114cda46). The catalogue estimates **0.007 billion m³ = 7 million m³** of flared gas in 2024 from 121 clear observations; the [NSTA monthly field reports](https://www.arcgis.com/home/item.html?id=dd38204275a04618ab7ddd00f87224e3) sum to **10.545 million m³** for the Mariner field in the same year.

That independent satellite/operator comparison is interesting, but it does **not** show hidden or illegal flaring. The estimates have different methods and possibly different source/reporting boundaries. The open flare-specific catalogue is annual; the nightly Nightfire download we tested required a login. Generic FIRMS showed only **two** heat detections within 5 km of Mariner in three recent seven-day satellite feeds, and the public Carbon Mapper plume query returned **zero** methane plumes in its Mariner box. Thus the proposed “catch an under-reported anomaly as it happens” demo has a weaker live evidence path in the North Sea. The honest flaring version is an **independent annual audit of measurement differences**.

## Pitch-sized distinction

**PeatWatch:** “A fire map shows the heat on top. We add Scotland's field-measured peat depth and drainage condition to show where the hidden carbon store may be at risk. Every alert shows its evidence, and we test whether this changes inspection priority.”

**Flaring:** “We independently compare satellite-estimated flaring at named North Sea stacks with operator-reported volumes, then flag discrepancies for investigation.”

PeatWatch has the stronger combination of **unexpected local data, an explainable small-compute method, and independent historical outcome data** for this particular challenge. The current evidence supports a hackathon prototype and honest validation plan, not a claim of measured accuracy or a real-time peat combustion detector.

# PeatPulse: proposed build and validation plan

**Working proposal — 3 October 2026.** This is a research-backed plan for the CompSoc challenge, not a record of a built system or a final team decision. The deadline is 18:15 UK time today, so the core demo is deliberately small. [Challenge brief](brief.md) · [Earlier dataset checks](peat-vs-flaring.md)

## The one-sentence idea

Find **peatland patches that are drying unusually for their place and season**, especially when broad weather data would not make them obvious, then give a land manager a short, evidence-linked list of places to inspect before a fire starts.

PeatPulse is an **inspection aid**. It does not detect underground combustion, know the exact water-table depth at every pixel, predict where ignition will occur, or automatically notify emergency services. A dry patch is more vulnerable if an ignition source arrives; that is different from a measured probability of fire.

## Who would use it and what would they do?

- **Primary user:** peatland managers and restoration teams deciding where to inspect a large site, check blocked drains, or monitor water levels.
- **Secondary user:** wildfire-prevention teams deciding which vulnerable peat areas deserve attention during a dry period. Formal operational use would require their own review and validation.
- **Output:** a map plus a downloadable ranked inspection list. Each card has a patch/area, observation date, drying evidence, local peat/condition context, the reason it was flagged, data age, confidence/coverage, and a suggested _inspection question_ rather than a claim that a fire will occur.

Example of the intended wording, **illustrative until measured**: “This bog patch is drier than its usual seasonal radar pattern despite ordinary regional rainfall. A nearby field survey records deep, artificially drained peat. Check water level and drainage on site.” Never replace “nearby” with “at this pixel” when the field point is some distance away.

## Why this fits the challenge

The [CompSoc brief](brief.md) asks for under-reported or easy-to-miss environmental anomalies, open imaging and sensor data, technical specifics, a live demo, small compute, and verifiable results against a baseline. **Localized abnormal drying** is the anomaly. The interesting question is whether the radar signal adds information beyond weather alone. The product turns that result into an inspection decision.

## The analysis pipeline

1. **Choose a test site with actual water-table loggers.** Start at Forsinard, where direct logger files and a Scottish Sentinel-1 study exist. Determine exact overlapping dates, coordinates and radar passes before drawing a map. If there are too few matched points, use another Peatland ACTION monitored site with downloadable data.
2. **Build a fair weather-only baseline.** For every matched logger/date, compute a fixed trailing rain-minus-evapotranspiration measure and season. A simple regression or percentile rule estimates whether the water table is unusually deep. Use only past/current observations available by that date.
3. **Extract radar features.** Use calibrated VV/VH backscatter from comparable acquisition mode, pass and relative orbit; avoid mixing them casually. Aggregate over a sensible neighborhood around each logger, mask obvious water/forest change or missing data, and compute seasonal deviations and recent trend. A 10 m raster grid is **pixel spacing**, not proof of independently resolved 10 m underground moisture. [Copernicus GRD guidance](https://documentation.dataspace.copernicus.eu/APIs/SentinelHub/Data/S1GRD.html) and [NatureScot report limitations](https://www.nature.scot/doc/naturescot-research-report-1362-developing-toolkit-monitoring-success-peatland-restoration-projects).
4. **Test weather + radar against weather alone.** Start with a small, interpretable ridge/linear model or robust score, using the same training/test dates and alert budget. Do not hard-code “a −2.5σ drop means peat is dry”: backscatter also responds to vegetation, surface water and viewing geometry. Learn the useful direction from training data and ground-check it.
5. **Make a separate inspection-priority layer.** Rank confirmed drying anomalies higher when dated nearby surveys show deep peat, drainage/erosion, or a restoration footprint that invites a check. Add InSAR condition only if time, coverage and temporal validity permit. Show _why_ a patch was elevated and when a contextual layer is missing. Keep **drying evidence**, **potential peat consequence**, and **human access** visible as separate components rather than hiding them in an uncalibrated “fire probability”.
6. **Produce the demo artifact.** Small map with a weather-only view, PeatPulse view, two adjacent patch cards, time-series plot against measured logger depth, and a CSV/GeoJSON inspection list. Run the statistical scoring on a laptop from small cropped inputs; image acquisition/preprocessing may depend on external services. Do not claim end-to-end Raspberry Pi performance without timing it.

### The two unusual experiments

- **“Dry despite rain” / matched bog twins:** compare a patch with its own seasonal radar history **and a nearby peat patch with similar cover, observed on the same satellite pass**. Subtract the control patch's change from the target patch's change. Both experienced broadly similar weather, so a large difference is a candidate local anomaly. Use the same simple math on older dates to avoid flagging places that always differ. Field loggers decide whether the signal is real; terrain, vegetation and SAR artifacts are alternate explanations. Test this paired score against the ordinary radar + weather model rather than assuming it helps.
- **“Dry despite restoration”:** intersect verified drying anomalies with **dated** restoration footprints and older drainage-condition surveys. The output is “check whether drainage blocking/re-wetting is functioning here,” not “the restoration failed.” This is a direct task for the land manager.

The long-term **bog-breathing** class is the stretch insight: a dynamic peat surface may respond differently from stiff or degrading peat during the same dry spell. Test it as an explanatory/priority factor, and drop it if it adds noise or has incompatible dates. A path/access layer may help route inspections or target prevention messaging, but is not a moisture predictor.

## The benchmark and proof standard

**Ground truth:** instrumented water-table depth at matched radar overpass dates. Define “unusually dry” from each logger/site's **training-period seasonal distribution** (for example, its deepest-water-table decile), before examining test dates. Fix thresholds using training data only.

**Primary comparison:** weather-only baseline versus the same model plus Sentinel-1 features. Both must see the same dates/sites and be allowed the **same number of alerts**. Report (a) dry observations caught at a fixed alert budget, (b) false alerts, and (c) water-table-depth error in cm if we predict depth. Compare with a SAR-only variant to show whether fusion helps. This is the real apples-to-apples benchmark.

**Split:** hold out the latest suitable period, with all processing and thresholds fit on earlier dates. If there are enough monitored sites, also hold out an entire site to test transfer. Never randomly scatter adjacent records from one logger across train and test; the [NatureScot report](https://www.nature.scot/doc/naturescot-research-report-1362-developing-toolkit-monitoring-success-peatland-restoration-projects) found that produced overly optimistic R²≈0.86. Clearly state sample sizes and coverage.

**Reference, not direct benchmark:** the published NatureScot model (6 cm MAE; time-split R²≈0.72; geographically held-out R²≈0.45) used a different dataset and protocol. We may cite it as prior work, but should not claim we “beat” it unless we reproduce its evaluation on the same data. The Met Office FSI measures potential fire severity if ignited, so its numerical score should not be compared with water-table prediction error.

**Decision gate:** claim a useful drying detector only if the held-out test beats the weather-only baseline at the same alert budget **without a flood of false alerts**. If radar is weak or cannot be acquired today, show a clearly labelled retrospective prototype based on weather + field data, with the satellite component marked unverified. Do not claim early fire prediction or avoided emissions from a water-table result.

## Honest limits to display in the product

- Radar backscatter is a **proxy** influenced by vegetation, surface roughness, standing water and acquisition geometry; it is not direct subsurface moisture. The effective Sentinel-1 resolution is coarser than 10 m pixel spacing.
- A satellite only revisits when it revisits. “Early” means before ignition **if** a drying anomaly is visible in time; it is not a guaranteed lead time or continuous monitor.
- Water-table loggers are sparse and point-based. Field depth/condition samples are uneven, often old, and may not describe the entire radar patch. Restoration records show work completed, not confirmed hydrological success.
- The InSAR class is long-term context and can be ambiguous on slopes or after recent restoration. A historical test must exclude contextual datasets created from future observations.
- Inspection priority is **not** calibrated fire probability. Ignition, spread and peat carbon loss require separate evidence. No automatic authority alerts in the hackathon demo.
